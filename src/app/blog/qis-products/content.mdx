*An empirical study of inverse-volatility exposure, daily-reset leverage and the February 2018 collapse.*

**Research data:** Bloomberg daily historical series, January 2011–November 20, 2026  


The appeal of systematic risk premia is easy to understand. Find a return that appears to recur, express it in a rules-based product and let compounding do the work. But when a strategy earns small gains by absorbing a rare, potentially enormous loss, its most impressive backtest may also conceal its greatest weakness.

XIV, the Credit Suisse-issued inverse-volatility exchange-traded note that collapsed in February 2018, is one of the clearest examples. Its underlying economic exposure was not a short position in the spot VIX. It was approximately **-1× the *daily* return of a rolling portfolio of near-term VIX futures**, subject to the ETN's terms and expenses. Those distinctions—*futures* rather than spot, and *daily reset* rather than long-horizon inverse performance—are the entire story.

Using daily Bloomberg histories for XIV, SVXY, the S&P 500 VIX Short-Term Futures Excess Return Index (`SPVXSP`), VIX and VIX futures, I asked two questions: **How attractive was the trade before its worst day? And what changes if inverse exposure is reduced from -1× to -0.5×?**

## 1. A remarkable return stream with an extraordinary left tail

From January 3, 2011, to February 2, 2018, XIV's Bloomberg market-price series increased from **$12.38 to $115.55**—an approximately **833% gain** for a hypothetical holder over that particular historical window, ignoring transaction costs. The price chart displays why the product became attractive: a long period of strong compounding, interrupted by sharp but apparently recoverable drawdowns.

![Growth of $100 across XIV, modeled daily inverse exposures and S&P 500 total return](/images/blogs/qis-xiv/01_rise_and_collapse.png)

To disentangle the product from its market price, I modeled two simple daily-reset strategies against `SPVXSP`:

<div style={{ textAlign: "center", margin: "1.5rem 0", fontSize: "1.08rem", fontWeight: 500, lineHeight: 2 }}>
  R<sub>t</sub><sup>(L)</sup> = −L × (I<sub>t</sub> / I<sub>t−1</sub> − 1) − 0.01 / 252, &nbsp; L ∈ {"{1, 0.5}"}
</div>

Here, <code>I_t</code> is the futures **excess-return** index, and the 1% annual drag is an *illustrative equal expense assumption* rather than either product's exact contractual fee. Wealth is compounded daily. The model excludes financing, trading slippage, market-price deviations, issuer credit risk and fund-closure mechanics; it is not a replication of XIV or SVXY.

Through February 2, 2018, the simplified **-1× model gained 793.8%**, versus **346.3% for -0.5×**. The larger position compounded much faster—but its maximum drawdown was already **74.8%**, compared with **47.0%** for half exposure. Even before the decisive event, this was not an ordinary low-risk carry trade.

## 2. The day that broke the strategy

On **February 5, 2018**, the Bloomberg `SPVXSP` index surged **96.10%** in a single day. For a daily-reset inverse model, that maps mechanically to a **-96.10% gross daily return at -1×**, or **-48.05% at -0.5×**. The same underlying shock has radically different implications for capital preservation depending on exposure.

The compounding arithmetic is brutal. The simulated -1× strategy's cumulative return from January 2011 changed from **+793.8% before the shock to -65.2% immediately afterward**. For the -0.5× model, the corresponding numbers changed from **+346.3% to +131.8%**. These results do not assume the half-exposure position is immune to crashes; they simply measure a lower proportional loss on this particular day.

But the Bloomberg *market closing prices* tell an especially instructive, and initially puzzling, story:

This is **not evidence that XIV escaped its February 5 economic loss**. It reveals that exchange closing-price series, index settlement calculations and indicative values can describe different clocks. Credit Suisse announced that XIV's *intraday indicative value* on February 5 had fallen to **20% or less of the prior day's closing indicative value**, triggering an acceleration event. The announced valuation and payment process was governed by the note's own terms, not by an ETF-style guarantee to investors. ([Credit Suisse announcement, SEC filing](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1053092/000095010318001572/dp86358_ex9901.htm))

A U.S. appellate decision records the later termination of the XIV notes and a **$5.99 final payment per note**. The Bloomberg February 15 exchange closing price in our sample is **$6.04**, reinforcing why the market price, indicative value and legal payout must not be treated as interchangeable. ([Second Circuit decision](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/us-2nd-circuit/2124632.html))

For researchers, this is a general lesson in product-level QIS analysis: **a daily close-to-close price backtest is not necessarily a daily close-to-close risk accounting of the underlying strategy during a discontinuous event**.

## 3. Did halving exposure solve the problem?

XIV ended. SVXY survived, but its risk objective changed. ProShares announced that SVXY would switch from seeking **-1×** to **-0.5×** the daily VIX short-term futures benchmark, effective after the close of February 27, 2018 (so the new exposure applied from February 28). ([ProShares SEC announcement](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1415311/000119312518059052/d503117dex991.htm))

That created a useful natural comparison. I ran the two simplified models over the same post-change index sample, **February 28, 2018–November 20, 2026**. This is a controlled scenario comparison—not a claim that investors could hold XIV after its termination.

Halving inverse exposure substantially reduced the volatility and drawdown of this simple backtest. But **a 62% drawdown remains severe**. Moreover, a -0.5× daily objective is *not* the same thing as a permanently short half-sized position: daily resetting, volatility of returns, market path and fees all determine multiday performance. The actual SVXY fund explicitly cautions that returns over periods other than a day can diverge materially from its stated daily multiple. ([ProShares SVXY product page](https://www.proshares.com/our-etfs/strategic/svxy))

A useful cross-check is that observed SVXY daily market-price returns after the objective change have an estimated sensitivity of roughly **-0.49** to contemporaneous `SPVXSP` returns, close to the advertised -0.5 daily target. That is a descriptive market-price regression, not a verification of exact NAV tracking.

## 4. Is contango the whole explanation?

The short-volatility thesis is often summarized as *selling expensive insurance*. In quiet markets, front-month volatility futures frequently sit below longer-dated contracts, and a continuously rolling **long** VIX futures index may face persistent adverse carry. An inverse product can benefit from negative index returns.

But a term-structure slope is not itself a realized trading return. To investigate rather than assume the connection, I used the Bloomberg first- and second-month VIX futures series to calculate <code>UX2_t / UX1_t − 1</code>, sorted observations into slope quartiles, then examined the following 20-trading-day *compounded* modeled -0.5× return.

![Term structure quartiles and subsequent twenty-day modeled returns](/images/blogs/qis-xiv/04_curve_quartiles.png)

The mean forward modeled returns were approximately **1.7%, 0.9%, 0.8% and 2.1%** across quartiles from flatter/backwardated to steeper contango. **They were not monotonic.** These windows overlap, and the calculation does not control for volatility regimes, changing risk premia or futures roll implementation. The evidence here does **not** support using a simple two-contract slope as a stand-alone predictive trading rule.

That negative result is useful. It suggests short-volatility performance reflects a mixture of futures risk premia, daily reset, convexity during shocks and path dependence—not merely the curve's shape on a particular day.

## 5. What this means for QIS product design

Three lessons stand out.

**The payoff distribution matters more than the smoothness of the cumulative-return chart.** A strategy can compound for years and still lose most of its capital in a single session. Standard annualized return and Sharpe estimates should be paired with drawdowns, stress tests, single-day loss distributions and barrier-event analysis.

**Product terms are part of strategy risk.** XIV was an unsecured ETN with an acceleration mechanism; SVXY is an ETF with a daily target and different operational and legal structure. A backtest of index returns cannot fully represent a product's indicative value, tradability, corporate actions, fees or mandatory redemption mechanics. ([XIV offering document](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1053092/000089109210005645/e41214fwp.htm))

**Reducing exposure helps, but does not abolish the short-volatility tail.** A half-sized inverse allocation would have preserved more wealth through the February 2018 futures shock, and its historical modeled post-2018 drawdown was meaningfully shallower. Still, a strategy that can lose more than 60% from a peak is not a defensive substitute for cash or bonds.

The full-period modeled drawdown comparison reinforces how heavily the choice of inverse exposure influences the strategy's tail-risk profile:

![Simulated maximum drawdown paths for inverse VIX futures exposure](/images/blogs/qis-xiv/05_drawdown_2011_2026.png)

The larger lesson extends beyond volatility ETNs. Any rules-based QIS product whose attractive carry is compensation for adverse convexity or liquidity stress deserves two separate analyses: **the return stream investors observe in normal markets, and the product mechanics that govern what happens when normal markets disappear**.

### Methods and limitations

- Data: Bloomberg `PX_LAST` for `XIV US Equity`, `SVXY US Equity`, `VXX US Equity`, `SPVXSP Index`, `SPVXSTR Index`, `VIX Index`, `SPXT Index`, and `UX1`/`UX2 Index`, as supplied for this analysis. XIV's available history ends in February 2018; the downloaded VXX history begins in January 2018.
- Simplified simulation: <code>R_t = −L × R_SPVXSP,t − 0.01/252</code>, daily compounded, for <code>L = 1</code> or <code>0.5</code>. No leverage constraint aside from the specified multiple, no intraday simulation, no management of margin/funding or collateral return, no execution slippage, no reconstitution of the actual indicative value, and no termination policy in the full-sample hypothetical paths.
- Percentages shown are from the uploaded data and locally computed models; they are **not** historical investable performance of a hypothetical surviving XIV. Actual SVXY prices are distinct from modeled results; corporate-action adjustment conventions should be confirmed in Bloomberg before any public replication.
- Slope-quartile forward returns use overlapping 20-day periods and are descriptive only; do not interpret them as statistical significance or a causal predictor.
